You Appointed the Officer. Nobody Changed the Quota.

Three quarters of companies hired a Chief AI Officer this year. Half of them took money off the sales floor to pay for it. And not one of those decisions changed what a seller is paid for, when commission lands, who owns the renewal, or what the price list says.

The Agentic Commercial Model Newsletter · Fessal Rahman · November 5, 2026 · 8 min read

From FR Advisory.

The author spent twenty years running commercial organisations inside software and private equity portfolios. He no longer works in the technology industry, holds no position in any company named in this series, has no clients in it, and is selling nothing to anyone in it.

Start where the money is actually made, because almost nobody writing about enterprise AI does.

A seller walks in on the first of January. Their quota is a contract value number. Their commission pays at signature. The renewal sits on somebody else's number. The price list in front of them is the same shape it was two years ago.

Now tell that person the company has been transformed by artificial intelligence.

They will nod, because they are polite, and then they will go and sell exactly what they are paid to sell, which is a large contract signed before the end of the quarter. They are not being obstructive. They are reading the only document that has ever mattered, which is the compensation plan, and it still says the same thing it said before.

That is the whole of Episode One. Everything underneath it, pricing, metering, revenue architecture, org design, exists to explain why that seller's year did not change.

What the company did instead

In 2025, 26% of organisations had a Chief AI Officer. In 2026, 76% do.

That is IBM's number, from a survey of two thousand chief executives across thirty three countries and twenty one industries, run with Oxford Economics between February and April. A fifty point jump in twelve months, and almost certainly the fastest addition to the executive floor in corporate history.

The same study, same chief executives. By their own estimate, 25% of their workforce uses AI regularly as part of the job.

Three quarters of companies appointed an executive. One quarter of employees opened the product.

The sales floor paid for it

This is the part that should land hardest with anyone carrying a number.

In March, ResumeBuilder surveyed 866 United States business leaders at director level and above. 54% said their company had reduced or would reduce employee compensation during 2026 specifically to free up capital for AI spending. Including those cutting jobs instead, it reaches 58%.

Where it came from: 61% took it out of bonuses. 60% out of equity. 59% out of raises. 53% out of benefits. 43% out of base salary. A quarter made people redundant.

Bonuses and equity are not abstractions in a revenue organisation. They are the variable pay of the people who bring in the money.

So compensation did change this year. It got smaller.

What compensation rewards did not move an inch. The quota is still contract value. Commission still lands at signature. Customer success is still measured on renewal rather than on what the customer actually realised. A smaller version of the wrong incentive is still the wrong incentive, and now the people carrying it are worse off and no better directed.

The four levers, and who holds them

Four decisions determine whether any of this shows up in revenue.

What the quota is. When commission pays. Who owns the renewal. What the price list says.

Every one of those sits inside the revenue organisation. The chief revenue officer, the chief financial officer, sales operations, the deal desk. Not one of them sits with the executive that 76% of companies just appointed.

This is the structural problem nobody states plainly. A Chief AI Officer is given pilots, platform selection, governance and an adoption target. They are not given the comp plan. They cannot move commission off signature. They cannot take the renewal off someone else's number. They cannot rewrite the value metric.

The single role created to drive the transformation is the only role in the building with no authority over the four things that decide whether it produces money.

That is not a people problem. It is a scoping decision, made deliberately, because the alternative would have meant handing an unproven function control over the forecast.

Pricing is one of four, and the only one anyone touched

Notice which of those four levers the market has been willing to move.

In one week in October a pricing tracker recorded two hundred and thirty pricing changes across two hundred and twenty two companies. Twenty were price rises. Roughly two hundred were something else: allowances cut, credits introduced, entitlements quietly reduced while the number on the page held.

Two hundred and twenty two companies rewrote a pricing page in a single week. In that same week, essentially nobody changed what the sales force was paid for, who owned the renewal, or when commission landed.

The price list is the only one of the four levers that can be changed by someone who does not have to call the chief revenue officer first. It lives on a web page. It requires no conversation about the forecast, no redesign of quota, no renegotiation with the sales leadership team.

So that is the one that moves. Not because it is the most important, but because it is the cheapest.

Pricing is a subset of this problem. It is treated as the whole of it because it is the only part that can be done unilaterally.

IBM's own study says the appointment is not the answer

Here is the finding that got no coverage, and it is the most useful thing in the report.

Organisations that redesigned five core business areas are four times more likely to have delivered on their business objectives.

Compare that to what the appointment buys. Organisations with an AI-first approach to executive design scaled about 10% more AI initiatives than their peers. Ten per cent more initiatives, which is a measure of activity rather than of return.

Four hundred per cent for rebuilding the business. Ten per cent for rebuilding the org chart above it.

The study that gave everyone the Chief AI Officer headline contains, several pages later, the evidence that the Chief AI Officer is not the lever. Nobody quoted that part, because redesign is slow, contested and nobody can be appointed to it on a Tuesday.

Why sensible people keep choosing this

It would be comfortable to call this stupidity. It is not, and treating it as stupidity is why most advice on the subject is useless.

Appointing an executive is fast, visible, announceable and reversible. It survives a board meeting and costs one salary. Cutting variable pay to fund technology is also fast, and carries the additional merit of looking like financial discipline while you do it.

Rewriting the quota is none of those things. It means the chief revenue officer accepts a disrupted year. It means finance accepts a different revenue shape. It means somebody tells the top performers that the thing they are brilliant at is no longer the thing that pays. It means a quarter, possibly two, where the numbers look worse before they look better.

One of those options is available immediately. The other costs somebody their forecast.

Taken individually, every quarter, the sensible decision is the one that changes nothing. That is a system working as designed, which is why it keeps producing the same result and will keep producing it until the money stops.

Three questions for your next revenue review

You do not need an audit to know whether your programme is real. You need three answers, and none of them involve a model or a platform.

What is the quota measured in, and is it different from two years ago. If it is still contract value, the sales organisation has been told, in the only language it reads, that nothing has changed.

When does commission pay, and against what. If it still lands at signature, nobody in the field has any reason to care what happens after the contract is signed, which is precisely where consumption and outcome revenue now lives.

Who has the authority to change either of those. If it is not the person running your AI programme, then your AI programme cannot produce a commercial result, and no amount of adoption or enthusiasm will rescue it.

The position

Seventy six per cent of companies appointed a Chief AI Officer this year. Fifty four per cent took money out of bonuses, equity and raises to pay for it. Twenty five per cent of the workforce uses the thing.

Meanwhile the quota is unchanged, commission still pays at signature, and the renewal still belongs to somebody who is not in the room when the deal is done.

You funded the technology by taking money off the sales floor, and then you left the sales floor pointed at exactly the same target it had before.

The motor is bolted on. The shafting is exactly where it was.
