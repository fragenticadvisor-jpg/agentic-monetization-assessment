Your Pipeline Starts Two Steps Too Late.

Your pipeline says the deal started when the form was filled in. It started weeks earlier, when somebody inside the account noticed a constraint and asked a model to help them name it. By the time you appear, the category is chosen and the shortlist is forming.

Value-Realised Selling · Fessal Rahman · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

From FR Advisory.

Every sales methodology ever written starts in the same place. Find the pain.

Discovery questions, pain funnels, the spin, the probe, the double click. Thirty years of training material built on one assumption, which is that the seller arrives before the problem has been named, and that the skill is in naming it with them.

That assumption is now wrong, and it is wrong in a way that your pipeline cannot see.

What actually happens before you exist

Somebody inside the account notices a constraint. A cost that has drifted, a team that cannot keep up, a board question nobody can answer.

What they do next used to be a search, a colleague, or an analyst report. Now, overwhelmingly, it is a prompt.

Semrush surveyed 643 United States business professionals in March and April of this year, 622 valid responses, with 54% of them the final decision maker in their buying group and another 41% part of the deciding group. These are not interns doing desk research.

Of those who use AI at work, 72% use it for early research and category scoping. That is the single highest-usage stage in the entire buying journey. Higher than comparing vendors at 62%, higher than narrowing the shortlist at 48%, higher than supporting the final decision at 45%.

The moment the problem gets a name is now the most AI-assisted moment in the whole process.

And by the time it has a name, it has a category. By the time it has a category, it has a candidate set. 92% say AI has shaped their vendor shortlist, and 45% of those say the effect was significant. 97% say it helped them discover a vendor they did not previously know.

So the first commercial act in the deal, deciding what kind of thing is being bought, has already happened. You were either in that answer or you were not.

The number that should worry your sales leadership

Here is the one that reframes everything about how the top of your funnel is measured.

After encountering a vendor through AI, 71% then visit that vendor's website. 63% then run a search. 38% then check review sites.

Read that from the inside of your own systems.

Your attribution records the website visit. Your form captures the website visit. Your reporting calls that visit the start of the relationship and attributes it to organic, or direct, or paid, depending on how they arrived.

For most of those buyers, the website visit is the second step, not the first. The decision about which vendors were worth visiting was made somewhere your systems cannot observe, by something that will never fill in a form.

Your first-touch attribution is measuring the second touch and calling it the beginning. Every pipeline review you run is built on a source field that is now structurally wrong.

That is not a marketing problem to be absorbed by marketing. It is the reason your sales leadership cannot explain why pipeline quality varies, why some quarters produce well-framed deals and others produce tyre-kickers, and why the channel mix stopped predicting anything useful about a year ago.

What this does to the seller

A seller arrives at a qualified lead believing the discovery conversation is where the problem gets defined.

It is not. It is where the problem gets confirmed. The buyer has already decided what sort of thing they are buying, roughly what it should cost, and which three names are credible. The seller's job in that conversation is no longer to uncover the pain. It is to find out how the pain was framed, by whom, and whether the framing favours them.

Those are different questions and almost nobody is trained to ask them.

What was the trigger. When did it fire. Who inside the business owns the problem now. How was the category decided. What comparison set are we in, and who put it together.

A seller who walks in and asks what keeps you up at night is asking a question the buyer answered three weeks ago, with a machine, in more detail than they will bother to repeat.

You cannot generate the trigger

This is the uncomfortable part for anyone running demand generation.

The constraint that starts the deal happens inside the customer's business, for reasons that have nothing to do with you. No campaign causes it. No sequence accelerates it. The trigger fires when it fires.

What you can control is whether you are the answer when it does.

That is a different discipline from the one most go to market teams are staffed for. It is not persuasion. It is being present, specific and legible at the moment somebody describes a problem to a model and asks what sort of thing solves it.

Which makes 66% have noticed vendors missing from AI results the most actionable number in the study. Two thirds of buyers have observed an absence. They know the answer they got was incomplete, and they proceeded with it anyway, because the shortlist was good enough.

Absence is not neutral. It is a silent disqualification that generates no record, no rejection, and no feedback. You will never see the deals you were not in.

What underpins this

The sales consequence is the headline. Underneath it sit three layers that have to move before anything changes.

Go to market. Demand generation is still built for a search box and a form. The material that wins the framing moment is published, structured and specific, with the numbers in the text rather than behind a gate. A gated asset is invisible to the thing now doing the shortlisting.

Commercial systems. The source field is wrong, so the reporting built on it is wrong. Until the model of how deals originate is updated, the sales organisation will keep optimising against a map that no longer matches the ground.

What the top of the funnel is measured on. If the team is paid and judged on form fills, they will keep manufacturing form fills, which is a measure of the second step. The measurement has to move upstream to presence in the framing, even though presence is harder to count than a conversion.

None of those are technology decisions. All three are commercial operating model decisions, which is the argument this entire series rests on.

What to do about it, concretely

Ask the trigger question in every discovery call. Not what are your challenges. What happened, and when, that made this a priority now. The answer tells you how much time has already passed and how fixed the framing is.

Find out who framed it. If a model named the category, ask what it said. Buyers will tell you, and the answer tells you precisely what you are being compared against.

Audit your own absence. Describe the problem your product solves, as a customer would describe it, and see what comes back. Do it for your three strongest use cases. If you are not in the answer, that is your top of funnel problem, and no amount of campaign spend addresses it.

Stop calling the form fill the start. Change the language in your pipeline reviews before you change the system. The deal did not start when they arrived. It started when something broke.

The position

The first stage of the customer's process is the one you have the least visibility into and the least control over, and it is now the stage most shaped by AI.

Seventy two per cent scope the category with a model. Ninety two per cent let it shape the shortlist. Seventy one per cent only reach your website afterwards, which means the moment your systems call the beginning is already the middle.

You cannot generate the trigger. You can only be the answer when it fires.

Everything after this stage is a negotiation inside a frame that somebody else built.
