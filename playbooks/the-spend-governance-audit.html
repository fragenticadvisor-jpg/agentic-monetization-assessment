Why this playbook exists
Consumption and outcome pricing only work if what's being consumed is actually measured accurately and governably. An agent that can take dozens of actions per task changes the shape of the metering problem entirely: the unit that needs governing is no longer a seat or even a simple API call, it's a chain of actions whose cost and value both need to be visible in real time, to you and to the customer being billed for it.
Two ways companies handle agent spend, compared

Only one of these survives a customer's finance team asking exactly what they're being charged for.

Approach 1
Unmetered Access
Agents run with broad access, usage tracked loosely or after the fact
Fast to ship — no metering infrastructure required upfront
No friction for the customer in the moment
Neither you nor the customer can answer what specifically drove this month's bill
Cost overruns are discovered after they've already happened
Approach 2
Governed Entitlement
Every agent action metered, capped, and attributable to a specific outcome
You and the customer can both see exactly what drove the number
Entitlement limits catch runaway spend before it becomes a bill dispute
Requires metering infrastructure built before the pricing model can safely go live
Needs ongoing investment as agent behaviour and action types evolve
Reality check
Blind Trust Billing
Usage-based price on paper, no real visibility underneath it
Looks like a modern consumption model from the outside
The first billing dispute exposes that no one can actually explain the number
Erodes trust faster than seat-based pricing ever did
Spend Governance Reality Check
Two questions to find out whether your metering can actually support the pricing model you're charging on.
Can you attribute this month's bill for any customer to specific, named agent actions?
Do customers have visibility into their own consumption before the invoice arrives?

Five signals your metering can't actually support the pricing model you're charging on

Mark each one honestly against your current billing and metering setup.

✓
You cannot attribute a customer's bill to specific, named agent actions without manual digging
If attribution requires an engineer pulling logs, it isn't a governance system — it's a forensic exercise you run after a dispute has already started.
✓
Customers see their consumption for the first time on the invoice, not before
A surprise invoice on a consumption model reads as a billing error even when the number is technically correct, because there was no visibility to build trust in it beforehand.
✓
There's no entitlement cap or alert before an account's usage spikes unexpectedly
Without a governance ceiling, a runaway agent loop becomes a customer's problem only when the bill arrives, not when the spike happens.
✓
Finance and product disagree on what unit is actually being metered
If the two functions closest to the pricing model can't agree on the unit, customers are very unlikely to understand it either.
✓
Metering infrastructure was bolted on after the pricing model launched, not built before it
Pricing announced ahead of the metering that has to support it is a common pattern, and it's usually where the first billing disputes come from.

The 30-day action plan
Week 1
Pick one account and trace their last bill. Try to attribute every line item to a specific, named agent action — note where the trail goes cold.
Week 2
Identify the visibility gap. Compare what you can explain internally to what the customer can see before the invoice arrives.
Week 3
Set entitlement guardrails. Define caps or alerts for at least your highest-risk usage pattern so spikes are caught before billing, not after.
Week 4
Give customers a consumption view. Even a simple dashboard or monthly summary ahead of invoicing closes most of the trust gap.
