Why this playbook exists
Salesforce has repriced Agentforce three times in eighteen months, per conversation, then per action, then back to per seat bundles, because nobody had settled what an agent's unit of value actually is before the pricing page went live. HubSpot layered credits onto seats and got a model customers called "too much" to manage, not too expensive. This playbook exists so you settle the metric question once, with evidence, before you publish anything.
The three metric types, compared

Every agentic pricing model reduces to one of three metric types. Most companies pick the first one that sounds intuitive without checking whether it will still make sense once the product gets better.

Type 1
Activity
e.g. per conversation, per action, per API call
Easy to instrument from day one
Intuitive to explain on a pricing page
Measures attempts, not results
Punishes you when the product gets more efficient
Type 2
Consumption
e.g. tokens, compute credits, seat hours
Scales naturally with usage volume
Familiar to buyers from cloud pricing
No ceiling for the buyer to budget against
Falls when customers optimise, even as value rises
Type 3 · Target state
Outcome
e.g. per resolution, per decision automated
Tracks what the customer actually recognises as value
Survives your product becoming more efficient
Requires real measurement infrastructure to verify
Harder to build than it is to describe

Five signals your metric is already failing

Mark each one honestly. These are the signals that show up before a repricing crisis, not after it.

✓
Your revenue on an account can fall while that customer's satisfaction rises
If a more efficient run of your product means a smaller bill, you're pricing activity, not outcomes, and you're being penalised on your income statement for making the product better.
✓
Finance can't forecast next quarter's revenue from this line without guessing
A metric with no floor and no ceiling isn't a pricing model, it's a liability. Enterprise buyers need a number for a spreadsheet; if finance can't produce one either, the model isn't done.
✓
Customers ask what the unit actually means more than once per sales cycle
If prospects keep asking "what counts as one conversation," the unit isn't legible. A metric that needs repeated explaining is a metric that isn't tracking real value.
✓
You've changed the pricing page more than once in the last twelve months
One change is iteration. More than one, without a new product to justify it, is usually evidence the unit of value was never actually settled in the first place.
✓
SMB accounts are quietly reducing usage when their budgets tighten
A metric with no committed floor invites cost optimisation the moment the macro turns. This is exactly what happened to early consumption pricing companies once growth era budgets tightened.

The 30 day validation plan
Week 1
Decompose current revenue by pricing metric and product line. You cannot design the next metric without knowing exactly what the current one is actually charging for.
Week 2
Run 5 willingness to pay conversations with your most agentic accounts. Ask what they'd expect to pay if priced by the outcome, not the activity: the gap between that answer and your current bill is your risk.
Week 3
Build the smallest possible outcome verification check for your top use case. It does not need to be complete, it needs to prove the outcome can be confirmed, not just attempted.
Week 4
Pressure test the model against your own efficiency roadmap. If the product gets 30% more efficient next quarter, does revenue on this metric rise, hold, or fall? If it falls, the model isn't ready to publish.
