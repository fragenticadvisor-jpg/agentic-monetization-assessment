Why this playbook exists
Net revenue retention was built for a seat based world, where more usage reliably meant more value and more expansion. Agentic products break that assumption: a customer can get dramatically more value while consuming less, because the product got more efficient at delivering the outcome. NRR alone can't tell the difference between a shrinking account and an account whose success just made your product need to do less work: that's the efficiency paradox, and it's why Value Realised Retention has to sit alongside NRR, not replace it, to see the whole picture.
Two ways to report revenue retention, compared

Only one of these can tell efficiency driven shrinkage apart from an account actually leaving.

Approach 1
NRR Only Reporting
Net revenue retention as the single north star retention metric
Familiar to every board and investor
Simple, single number to track quarter over quarter
Can't distinguish a healthy, efficient account from a churning one
Punishes exactly the outcomes an agentic product is supposed to deliver
Approach 2
NRR + Value Realised Retention
NRR tracked alongside a measure of actual value delivered per account
Separates efficiency driven revenue shrinkage from real churn risk
Gives the board the full picture instead of a number that can mislead either direction
Requires defining and tracking a new metric, which takes real work upfront
Harder to summarise in one number than NRR alone
Reality check
The Efficiency Paradox Trap
NRR dips as the product gets better, and it reads internally as a failure
The metric is easy to report and everyone already understands it
A genuinely successful account can look identical to a failing one on this metric alone
Teams can end up penalised for making the product too good at its job
NRR Truth Reality Check
Two questions to find out whether your revenue reporting can tell efficiency apart from churn risk.
Has any account's usage or spend declined while their actual outcomes or results improved?
Do you currently track a value realised metric alongside NRR, separate from revenue alone?

Five signals your NRR number is hiding the efficiency paradox

Mark each one honestly against your last two quarters of account reviews.

✓
You report NRR without a companion metric for value actually realised
NRR alone can't distinguish a shrinking but thriving account from a genuinely at risk one: without a second lens, both look the same on the board slide.
✓
Account reviews treat any usage decline as a red flag, without checking outcomes first
An account whose outcomes improved while usage fell doesn't need a rescue plan: treating it as churn risk misdirects CS effort toward a healthy account.
✓
Nobody has explicitly modelled what efficiency driven revenue shrinkage looks like for your product
Without a model of what this pattern looks like in your own data, it's indistinguishable from real risk when it actually appears.
✓
Product improvements that reduce required usage are not tracked against retention impact
If product and revenue teams don't share a view on this, product's own success can look like a revenue problem to the people reporting the numbers.
✓
Forecasting assumes stable or growing usage per account as the default case
A forecast that doesn't account for efficiency driven usage decline will consistently misread its healthiest, most successful accounts.

The 30 day action plan
Week 1
Pull accounts with declining usage over the last two quarters. Cross reference against outcome or satisfaction data to see which are efficiency gains versus real risk.
Week 2
Define a value realised metric. Pick a proxy specific to your product (outcomes delivered, problems resolved, time saved) that can sit alongside NRR.
Week 3
Re brief account reviews around both metrics. Make sure CS and revenue teams read usage decline through both lenses before triggering a save motion.
Week 4
Bring both numbers to your next board or leadership update. Show NRR alongside value realised retention so the full picture, not just one number, drives the conversation.
