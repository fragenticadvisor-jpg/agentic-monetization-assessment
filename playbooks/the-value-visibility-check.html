Why this playbook exists
Customer success built around managing renewals treats the renewal date as the moment of truth. But by the time a renewal conversation happens, the customer has already decided, quietly, weeks or months earlier, based on whether they could feel and point to the value they were getting. Calibration and switching-cost visibility have to be built and proven continuously, not reconstructed defensively in the week before a contract ends.
Two ways customer success is structured, compared

Only one of these catches an at-risk account while there's still time to act.

Approach 1
Renewal-Focused CS
CS engagement concentrated in the weeks before contract end
Efficient use of CS time — effort matches the calendar deadline
Clear, familiar structure to run and staff against
Finds out value wasn't realised only when it's too late to fix
No visibility into quiet decline until the renewal call itself
Approach 2
Value-Proving CS
Continuous tracking and proof of realised value, calibrated over time
Catches decline early enough to actually intervene
Renewal becomes a formality, not a negotiation, because value was never in doubt
Requires ongoing measurement infrastructure, not just a renewal playbook
Needs a clear definition of what value looks like for each account, which takes real work upfront
Reality check
Silent Erosion
Usage quietly declines, nobody notices until the renewal conversation
Looks calm — no alarms, no escalations, until suddenly there are
By the time it's visible, the customer has usually already decided
CS gets blamed for a decline they had no early warning of
Value Visibility Reality Check
Two questions to find out whether you'd catch an at-risk account in time to actually do something about it.
Can you currently name which accounts have had declining usage or engagement in the last quarter?
Do customers themselves have a way to see the value they're realising, independent of your renewal pitch?

Five signals value is eroding silently before your renewal conversations

Mark each one honestly against your last two renewal cycles.

✓
You can't name which accounts have had declining usage without pulling a special report
If usage decline isn't a standing, visible metric, it's discovered reactively, usually at the renewal conversation where there's no time left to act.
✓
Value is proven mainly through a renewal-time business review, not ongoing reporting
A single point-in-time pitch is a much weaker signal to a customer than value they've been able to see accumulate over the contract.
✓
CS engagement is concentrated in the weeks before contract end, not spread across the term
By the time CS engages heavily, the customer has typically already formed their view of whether the product is worth renewing.
✓
There's no defined 'value realised' metric specific to what each customer actually needed
Generic usage stats are a weak proxy for value — the customer's own definition of success is what actually predicts renewal.
✓
Switching cost is assumed rather than demonstrated to the customer
If the customer doesn't feel the switching cost themselves, a competitor's pitch at renewal time can look deceptively easy to accept.

The 30-day action plan
Week 1
Pull usage trends for your top-20 accounts by ARR. Flag any account with declining engagement over the last two quarters, not just the ones already escalated.
Week 2
Define value-realised for your three most common use cases. Write down, specifically, what success looks like so it can be measured, not just asserted.
Week 3
Build a lightweight ongoing report. Even a quarterly summary of realised value, sent proactively, beats a renewal-week business review.
Week 4
Move CS touch points earlier in the contract term. Shift effort from renewal-week firefighting to mid-term value checks where intervention is still possible.
