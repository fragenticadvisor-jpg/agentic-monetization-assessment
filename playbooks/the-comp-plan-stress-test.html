Why this playbook exists
When a company moves its pricing from seats to consumption or outcomes, the comp plan is usually the last thing to change, and the gap between the two is where the strategy quietly fails. A rep paid on new logo bookings has no incentive to protect the expansion and consumption growth the new pricing model depends on. Snowflake's own comp rebuild around consumption is the visible example of what happens when a company takes this seriously enough to redesign the plan itself, not just the pricing page.
Two ways comp plans respond to a pricing model change, compared

Only one of these actually pulls in the same direction as the pricing strategy.

Approach 1
Legacy Carryover
Pricing changes, comp plan stays built around bookings
No disruption to how reps are used to being paid
Simple to run: no plan redesign needed
Rewards new logo bookings while the business now depends on expansion and consumption
Reps have no incentive to protect the metric the strategy actually needs
Approach 2
Consumption Aligned Comp
Comp rebuilt around the same unit of value as the pricing model
Pulls reps toward the behaviour the strategy actually needs
Consumption and expansion get actively managed, not just hoped for
Requires a genuine plan redesign, not a tweak
Needs new reporting to track the new metric accurately
Reality check
The Silent Mismatch
Leaders assume alignment exists because pricing changed
Feels resolved: the pricing announcement implied the whole system moved
Comp keeps pulling toward the old behaviour even after pricing has moved on
Nobody notices until consumption growth stalls and no one owns why

Five signals your comp plan is pulling against your own pricing strategy

Mark each one honestly against your current plan.

✓
Reps are still paid primarily on new logo bookings while the business depends on expansion or consumption
A plan like this pays for the wrong outcome: the metric that actually drives the business goes unmanaged because no one is compensated for protecting it.
✓
No one can say what percentage of comp is tied to the new pricing metric versus the old one
If this number isn't known, the mismatch is invisible by default: you can't fix a gap you haven't measured.
✓
Consumption or usage decline post sale carries no consequence in the comp plan
If falling usage doesn't affect a rep's pay, nobody on the revenue team is incentivised to notice or act on it before renewal.
✓
The comp plan was last redesigned before the current pricing model existed
A plan built for a previous pricing era optimises for behaviour that no longer matches how the business actually earns revenue.
✓
Finance and sales leadership disagree on what the comp plan is currently rewarding
If the two functions that should be most aligned on this can't agree, the plan is very likely pulling in more than one direction at once.

The 30 day action plan
Week 1
Map what comp currently rewards. List the top three behaviours your current plan actually pays for, regardless of what it was designed to reward.
Week 2
Compare that map to your pricing model's unit of value. Identify the specific gaps where comp and pricing are pulling in different directions.
Week 3
Draft the realignment. Redesign the highest impact gap first: usually the treatment of expansion, consumption, or renewal risk.
Week 4
Pilot with one team before a full rollout. Test the redesigned plan with a subset of reps and check behaviour actually shifts before changing it company wide.
