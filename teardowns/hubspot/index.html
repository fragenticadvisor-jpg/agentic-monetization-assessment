Agentic Layer Check

HubSpot

HubSpot has published something almost nobody else in this category will: a rate card. Fifty credits to resolve a conversation, 100 to recommend a lead, 500 to produce an invoice, at a cent a credit. Spend caps arrive switched on, and a customer can convert seats into credits mid contract without waiting for renewal. That last move solves a monetisation problem and creates a compensation one. Here's how it reads against the nine layers.

Agentic Layer Check · Public signal read · October 2026

What they sell

A full customer platform of hubs across marketing, sales, service, content and operations, now with Breeze agents (customer, prospecting, content, nurture, data, revenue, knowledge base) running on top of the same CRM.

Who they sell to

SMB through mid market and increasingly enterprise, self serve from Free and Starter, sales assisted at Professional and Enterprise, plus a large partner and agency channel reselling and implementing the platform.

How they price

Seats plus credits. Core Seats from $45/mo on Professional and $75/mo on Enterprise, with included credits of 500, 3,000 and 5,000 a month on Starter, Professional and Enterprise. Credits cost $0.010 each, bought in packs or on Pay-as-You-Go, and agent actions are published at fixed credit rates.

What's working

Two things here are a level above what the rest of this series has found, and both are on HubSpot's own public pages.

Layer 6 · Metering & Entitlement

A published rate card, priced per outcome

HubSpot states it plainly: "Credits are only consumed when specific actions are performed, so you only pay for what you use," and "You only pay when the work is done." Then it prints the rates. Fifty credits to resolve a conversation, 50 per voice minute, 100 to recommend outreach for one lead, 10 per nurture email, 10 per data response, 200 per knowledge base article, 500 per invoice, 1,000 per piece of content, at $0.010 a credit. That is a per outcome price list a buyer can model before signing, in a category where the normal answer is an opaque credit rate and a sales call. Every other teardown in this series has asked what one credit actually buys. HubSpot answers it on the pricing page, action by action.

Layer 9 · Commercial Systems

The controls are on before the customer asks

Spend controls ship switched on, not buried in a settings page for the finance team to discover after the first surprising invoice. HubSpot sets an account level credit limit up to 50% above a customer's monthly credits by default, notifies admins at 75%, 85%, 90% and at the limit, and allows caps per feature and per action on top. Pay-as-You-Go then bills usage beyond the monthly credits "only when the work gets done," in arrears, in ten credit increments. A ceiling, a warning ladder and an overflow valve, built into the billing stack rather than promised in a contract. Zapier built the valve and left it off the agent product. HubSpot built both and turned the ceiling on by default.

Where there's room to extend it

The flexibility that makes this good for the buyer lands hardest two layers away, on the people who sell it.

Layer 5 · Compensation · Opportunity

The customer can now dismantle the seat line mid contract

Per HubSpot's own announcement, customers "can swap seats for credits (or for another seat type) themselves without waiting for their contract to renew." Commercially that is the right call: it lets a customer fund agent usage out of a seat budget they are no longer using, without a renewal negotiation. However, seats are what sales is paid on. A self serve mid term conversion means contracted seat revenue can migrate into a usage line that quota, commission and territory plans were never written against, and it can happen in a month with no rep involved. The opportunity is a comp architecture that pays on total committed value and credit consumption growth rather than seat count, because the pricing model now lets the customer move money between the two whenever they like.

Layer 3 · Pricing & Packaging · Opportunity

Pay for what you use, except the part that lapses

Credits reset monthly and, per HubSpot's own billing documentation, unused credits lapse at the end of each period and do not carry forward. So the included 3,000 or 5,000 credits are a monthly commitment that expires, which sits awkwardly next to "you only pay for what you use" on the same company's pricing page. The defaults pull the same direction: at the account limit, features pause until reset or until more capacity is bought, only Super Admins or Billing Admins can switch overages on, and the feature and action level caps are off by default, with action level available for buyer intent alone. A customer who never opens billing settings therefore gets the ceiling and the pause, not the valve. Rolling unused credits even partially, as Lovable does for two months, would turn a lapse into a reason to keep expanding usage.

How this moves through the model

Both findings converge on Revenue Architecture (Layer 8). A seat line convertible into credits at the customer's discretion, over a credit balance that expires monthly, means annual contract value stops being a reliable shape: the same committed spend can sit in seats in January and in credits in March, with different margin and different predictability behind each. Forecasting now needs to model mix migration inside a live contract, not just renewal and expansion. It lands on Sales Strategy & Enablement (Layer 4) too, because a rep selling 5,000 credits has to explain a rate card where one resolved conversation costs 50 and one piece of content costs 1,000, which is a far more honest conversation than a credit bundle and a far harder one to run without training. HubSpot has done the hard part, pricing the work itself and showing the price. The rest of the operating model, comp, quota, forecasting and enablement, is what has to catch up.

Methodology & sourcing
