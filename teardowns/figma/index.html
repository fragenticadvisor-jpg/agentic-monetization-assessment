Agentic Layer Check

Figma

Figma sells seats. Its customers now spend on something else entirely. More than 80% of its paying customers above $10,000 a year were using up artificial intelligence allowances by the end of October, and two thirds were using the new assistant every week. The seller still closes a seat count. The usage that follows lands on a separate meter the buyer manages with an administrator, long after the deal is signed. On 4 November the company reported 43% growth, raised its outlook, and the shares fell anyway. Here's what that does to the commercial model, layer by layer.

Agentic Layer Check · Public signal read · November 2026

What they sell

Design and collaboration software used by product teams to design, prototype, build and hand off work, now with an assistant and build tools that generate and change work on request.

Who they sell to

Individual designers and small teams self serve, then design, product and engineering organisations buying by seat count, with larger companies on annual agreements and administrator controlled settings.

How they price

Per editor a month, $12 on Professional billed annually and $45 on Organization, with every seat carrying a monthly allowance for the assistant: 3,000 on a full Professional seat, 3,500 on Organization, 4,250 on Enterprise, and 500 on the lighter seat types. Administrators buy more for the team as a monthly package or as pay as you go.

What's working

Figma made the generous call twice, and both times it was the right one.

Layer 3 · Pricing & Packaging

Costs fell, so customers got more rather than a discount

In August the purchased allowances grew: twice as much on the Professional plan and 1.6 times as much on Organization and Enterprise, with Figma stating plainly that customers were getting more at their existing cost, and existing invoices adjusted so the total bill stayed the same while the amount included went up. That is the correct response to delivery costs falling. A price cut is permanent and invisible within a month. More capability for the same money raises what the customer gets out of the relationship and leaves the price intact, which is the difference between improving the exchange and simply giving the gain away.

Layer 6 · Metering & Entitlement

Routine work stays free, and the meter only counts the expensive part

Searching, renaming layers and tidying up notes cost nothing. Simple picture tasks carry a small fixed cost. Only the heavy, open ended work varies with the request. Figma also publishes the monthly allowance for every seat type, pools everything an administrator buys across the whole team, spends the cheaper package before the pay as you go rate, and lets administrators set a monthly ceiling and per person limits. For a buyer trying to work out what this will cost, that is a far more answerable question than most of this market allows.

Where there's room to extend it

The gap is not in the pricing. It is in what the person selling has to work with.

Layer 4 · Sales Strategy & Enablement · Opportunity

The seller closes seats while the value shows up on another meter

The public pricing page does not mention the allowances at all. A buyer has to leave it and go into the help pages to find out what a seat includes, which means the fastest growing part of the product is absent from the conversation where the deal is agreed. The seller commits a seat count, the usage arrives afterwards, and the first time anyone discusses it is when an administrator is deciding whether to buy more for the team. By then the renewal, not the rep, owns the outcome. Putting the allowance on the pricing page and into the deal, with a sizing conversation at the point of sale, turns a surprise into a planned expansion, and gives the person carrying the number something to sell beyond headcount.

Layer 8 · Revenue Architecture · Opportunity

The company proves sharing works, then withholds it from what the seat includes

Anything an administrator buys is pooled across the whole team. What comes with each seat is not: per Figma's own documentation it cannot be shared or transferred between people, it expires monthly, and it does not carry over. So the same company runs two rules on the same product, and the more generous one is the one you pay extra for. In practice the heaviest users run dry while quieter colleagues' allowances expire untouched, and the resolution is to buy a shared pool. Letting the included allowance pool across the team, or carry for a month, would turn the most common pattern in any team from a monthly write off into a reason to grow usage rather than a reason to ration it.

How this moves through the model

Both findings land first on Compensation (Layer 5). If the seat count is what gets closed and the allowance is what grows, then the number a seller is paid against stops tracking the value the customer is taking, and nobody is rewarded for the part of the account that is expanding fastest. It then lands on Revenue Architecture (Layer 8) in public: the quarter showed 43% growth to $391.4m with the outlook raised, and the shares still fell on the day, because usage growth in this shape costs money to serve and the market can see it arriving before the pricing has caught up. That is the whole argument of this series in one print. The technology worked. The adoption is real and measurable, with most large customers now using the allowances every month. What the market marked down was the commercial model around it, and the fix for that sits in the deal, the comp plan and the packaging, not in the product.
